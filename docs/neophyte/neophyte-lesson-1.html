01. The Interface Model

We reject the notion of Tarot as "fortune telling." Instead, we adopt the Interface Model. The deck is a database of 78 archetypal images. You are the operator. To use the system safely, you must define your inputs and constraints before you engage.

This model has deep roots in Western esotericism. When Éliphas Lévi declared in 1855 that "the Tarot is a monumental and extraordinary work," he was not speaking of divinationhe was describing a philosophical instrument. Lévi connected the 22 Major Arcana to the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet, and thus to the 22 paths on the Tree of Life. This was not arbitrary symbolism; it was systematic correspondence.

The Golden Dawn formalized this system in the 1880s, creating the most comprehensive mapping between Qabalah and Tarot that the Western tradition has known. S.L. MacGregor Mathers, Arthur Edward Waite, and later Aleister Crowley all built upon this foundation, though they diverged in interpretation. What they shared was the recognition that the Tarot is a language, not a oracle.

"The true Tarot is symbolism; it speaks no other language and offers no other signs."

 Arthur Edward Waite, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1911)

When you draw a card, you are not receiving a message from the universe. You are selecting a symbol and then performing a cognitive operation upon it. The randomness ensures you cannot predict which symbol will appearthus preventing your conscious mind from choosing only comfortable images. This is the technology of the draw.

02. The Anatomy of the Deck

The 78 cards of the Tarot divide into two primary subsystems: the Major Arcana (22 cards) and the Minor Arcana (56 cards). This division mirrors the structure of consciousness itself.

22

Major Arcana

The Atu or "Trumps"archetypal forces and states of consciousness. These are the 22 paths connecting the Sephiroth on the Tree of Life. Each corresponds to a Hebrew letter, an astrological attribution, and a specific stage of initiation.
